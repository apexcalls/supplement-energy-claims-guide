Supplement Claims About Energy: What the Label Must Actually Show

"Energy" is one of the most common words on supplement labels — and one of the least informative. A product can promise energy without containing anything that produces it, because the word itself is not a regulated claim in the way a vitamin content claim is. Knowing what the label must actually show separates a real energy product from a bottle of caffeine and adjectives.

First, check what provides the energy. Real energy support on a label usually means B vitamins, which help the body convert food into energy, or iron, where a deficiency causes tiredness. If the label's energy claim rests on caffeine alone, the product is a stimulant, not an energy supplement — it borrows alertness from tomorrow rather than supporting energy production today. The label should name the ingredient and its amount; a blend that hides amounts behind "proprietary" tells you nothing.

Second, check the dose against the evidence. A B vitamin listed at 25% of its daily value will not do much for energy, whatever the front of the bottle says. Compare each relevant dose with the amount used in research, not with the size of the claim on the front.

Third, read the warnings. Energy products often carry cautions about sleep, blood pressure, or interactions with medicines. A label that hides its warnings in the smallest print while shouting its benefits in the largest is telling you where its priorities lie.

Finally, check the testing. Does the brand name its laboratory and publish batch certificates? A brand that tests its products will say so in specific terms.
